FRC robots commonly have a mechanism that needs to be controlled to a precise setpoint, such as position or velocity.
One example is aiming your robot at a scoring goal.
Robots align themselves by measuring their current angle with a target angle, and adjusting until the two are equal.
Scoring with auto alignment is faster and more efficient than your driver lining up with the goal by hand.

In this section, we will cover the basics of using measurements from sensors on the robot to help position mechanisms precisely.
This type of control that incorporates feedback from sensors into the movement of the mechanisms is called a **feedback controller**.
The most common feedback controller in FRC, and the one you will be learning about in this section, is a PID Controller.

## Feedback Controller

PID is a type of feedback controller.
A feedback controller uses sensors and math to determine what the mechanism is currently doing and adjust its power to keep it on target.

The mechanism’s current state is called the **output**.
The target state that we want it to reach is called the **setpoint**.

For this stage, the kitbot’s auto align will drive a certain distance, then turn.
Let’s look at the driving part.
To make the kitbot drive a certain distance, we can use the kitbot’s drivetrain encoders.
Encoders are sensors that measure the motor’s rotation, which report how far the robot has driven.
In this example, the output is how far the robot has actually driven.
The setpoint is the target distance, such as 1 meter towards the goal.

Feedback controllers are also used outside of FRC.
One example is an air conditioner.
An air conditioner has a sensor that compares the current temperature with the desired temperature.
Then the air conditioner directs the airflow in a way which allows the air to get to the desired temperature.

This stage will use PID to help the kitbot auto align and drive towards the goal.

## PID

PID is an acronym that stands for **P**roportional, **I**ntegral, and **D**erivative.
These are the three gains that the controller sums together in order to calculate the output to the motor.
Together the three terms are P, I, and D, which is how PID gets its name.

PID works by constantly comparing the current output to the setpoint and changing the control effort, or in our case, the voltage, commanded to the motor.
The difference between the output to the setpoint is called the error.

- The Proportional (P) gain scales the output linearly with the error.
  The further away from the setpoint, the stronger the effect of the proportional gain.
  It is analogous to a spring.
- The Integral (I) gain scales with the sum of the error over time.
  The longer the mechanism spends not at the setpoint, the stronger the effect of the integral gain.
  It is often unused in FRC due to a side effect called _integral windup_.
- The Derivative (D) gain scales with the rate of change of the error.
  The faster your mechanism approaches the setpoint, the stronger the derivative term tries to slow it down.
  It is analogous to a dashpot,

In code, the three terms are commonly noted as `kp`, `ki`, and `kd`.
k is a common prefix for constants and coefficients.

<Aside type="note">
    The equation for PID is: `output = kP * error + kI * \integral(error dt) +
    kD * de/dt.` While it's helpful to use the equation to see how the terms add
    up and what the gains do, understanding the equation isn't required.
</Aside>

### How do P, I, and D work together?

Now that we know what P,I, and D are, lets look at how the three terms work together to reach a setpoint.
As PID are adjusted to reach the setpoint, the mechanism's output can act in different ways.
Two things to look out for are **overshooting** and **oscillating**.
When the output passes the setpoint, this is called overshoot.
When the output goes up, then down, up again, and repeats, this is called oscillating.

### Tuning PID

Tuning is the process of selecting the P, I, and D gains to achieve the desired mechanism control.

This process is necessary because every mechanism is different and behaves differently.
Even two theoretically identical robots may require different PID constants, as there may be small mechanical differences that impact how mechanisms behave.

To tune PID, the output and setpoint's information is put into a graph to help figure out how to tune each term.
When tuning PID, the output isn't always a clean line.
As the values are adjusted, the output line takes on different shapes which can help determine which value needs to be adjusted and how.

<Aside type="tip">
    When increasing PID values start small and increase carefully. Starting with
    huge numbers or increasing in big jumps can make it difficult to get the
    desired outcome.
</Aside>

When tuning PID, the first step is to set all three terms to 0.
The first value that you want to tune is P.
Increase P until the output is near the setpoint but it starts to oscillate.

If the output is close to the target but just misses it, I can be increased to push it towards the set point.
In FRC, this part is commonly skipped.

If there are any oscillations, D can be introduced.
Increasing D, can help smooth out the oscillations.
However, make sure that it isn't increased so much that it causes jittering.

Similar to the examples, graphs are used to tune PID.
By using a graph, we can see the mechanism's output, compare it to the setpoint, and adjust the different terms until
the output matches the setpoint.
