With FRC games, it's common for teams to have a mechanism move to a desired position or move the robot to a desired position or angle.
One example is auto aligning.
Robots auto align by turning a certain amount of degrees towards the goal then take the steps needed to score the game piece.
This helps makes scoring more efficient.
In 2026, the kitbot might auto align to the goal by turning to a certain angle on the field.
The kitbot can't turn too far to the left or too far to the right because then it might miss the goal.
It has to turn just the right amount.
How do teams make sure that happens?
It's with a feedback controller called PID.

## Feedback Controller

A feedback controller uses sensors and equations to figure out what the mechanism is currently doing,
and controls the mechanism's output to make it do what we want it do.
The mechanisms's current state is called the **output**.
The desired state of the mechanism, where we want it to be, is called the **setpoint**.

In the case of the kitbot's auto align, the **output** is the information from the sensor on what the robot's current angle is.
The **setpoint** is a target angle, perhaps pointing the kitbot towards the goal.

<Aside type="note">
    We're able to get the kitbot's angle through Systemcore's built in IMU, or
    Inertial Measurement Unit. This is a sensor that allows the robot to
    accurately track its orientation in 3D space, returning this in the form of
    3 angles, roll, pitch, and yaw.
</Aside>

Feedback controllers are also used outside of FRC.
One example is an air conditioner.
An air conditioner has a sensor that compares the current temperature with the desired temperature.
Then the air conditioner directs the airflow in a way which allows the air to get to the desired temperature.

A type of feedback controller is PID.
This stage will use PID to help the kitbot auto align and drive towards the goal.

## PID

PID is a type of feedback controller that uses three terms: proportional, integral, and derivative.
Together the three terms are P, I, and D, which is how PID gets its name.

PID works by constantly comparing the current output to the setpoint and changing the control effort, or in our case, the voltage, commanded to the motor.
The difference between the outpoint to the setpoint is called the error.

- Proportional (P) is what gets you close to the setpoint.
  It's proportional to the error.
- Integral (I) helps carefully creep the rest of the way to the setpoint.
  It's the integral of the error.
- Derivative (D) is a braking force to stop or “dampen” any oscillations.
  It's the derivative of the error

In code, the three terms are commonly noted as `kp`, `ki`, and `kd`.
The k stands for constants.

### How does P, I, and D work together?

PID is constantly comparing where you are to where you want to be and adjusting.
This is possible because each term is working to help adjust.
The example shows the three terms working to get the output to match the setpoint.

First we start off with tuning P.
As P increases, the output overshoots and oscillates.
Overshooting is when the output passes the setpoint, and oscillating means it passes the setpoint repeatedly.

### Tuning PID

Adjusting the three terms to get the desired output is called **tuning**.
Tuning PID is necessary because every mechanism is different and behaves differently.
Even two theoretically identical robots may require different PID constants, as they may be small mechanical differences that impact how mechanisms behave.

<Aside type="tip">
    When increasing PID values start small and increase carefully. Starting with
    huge numbers or increasing in big jumps can make it difficult to get the
    desired outcome.
</Aside>

When tuning PID, the first step is to set all three terms to 0.
The first value that you want to tune is P.
Increase P until the output is near the setpoint but it starts to oscillate.

If the output is close to the target but just misses it, I can be increased to push it towards the set point.
However, in FRC, this part is commonly skipped.

If there are any oscillations, D can be introduced.
By increasing D, it can help smooth out the oscillations.
However, make sure that it isn't increased so much that it causes jittering.

Similar to the examples, graphs are used to tune PID.
By using a graph, we can see the mechanism's output, compare it to the setpoint, and adjust the different terms until
the output matches the setpoint.
